Dear Editor, Associate Editor, and Reviewers,

Thank you for your careful assessment and constructive suggestions. We have revised the manuscript to clarify its efficiency comparisons and adaptive sensing mechanism, explain the adaptive reward, and improve the presentation of the simulation and hardware experiments.

The principal revisions are summarized below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We have specified the Gallant comparison: $98.6\%$ fewer per-frame pre-encoder exteroceptive values, approximately $56\%$ lower peak GPU memory, and $32.7\%$ of Gallant's training-iteration time under the stated training settings.
    \item We have clarified that adaptive perception uses radius smoothness and regularization rather than a speed- or obstacle-dependent target radius, and specified the reward formulas and weights in the detailed responses.
    \item We have clarified that the adaptive radius changes only the vertical distance map; the horizontal elevation map retains fixed coverage and sampling resolution, consistent with Sec.~II-B.
    \item We have specified that the adaptive-radius ablation in Table~V is a simulation experiment, identified which two reward terms are removed, and removed the Table~V comparison from Sec.~IV-C-1.
    \item We have revised Fig.~1 and Sec.~III-C to distinguish the actor from the privileged critic and show separate recurrent LSTM states. We have added approximate 95\% Wilson intervals for the pooled success outcomes in Fig.~5 (Sec.~IV-C-2), and corrected the typo in Sec.~I.
\end{itemize}

In the detailed response below, the Associate Editor's points are summarized and the reviewers' comments are reproduced with minor typographic normalization in \textcolor{reviewblue}{blue italics}; our responses appear in black. Each response identifies the relevant manuscript sections and figures or tables; clarifications provided only in this letter are explicitly distinguished from manuscript changes.

\subsection*{AE Comment 1: Evidence for compactness and computational efficiency}
\begin{reviewcomment}
Authors claim that ADAPT provides a more compact representation and potentially makes reinforcement learning more computationally efficient, compared with voxel-based alternatives. The authors should specify how the comparisons were conducted. They have to provide enough detail to verify both the dimensionality reduction and the training-speed comparison. In particular, R8 asks where the 56\% computational overhead reduction and the 98.6\% observation-dimensionality reduction are supported. R3 notes that Table IV reports only training efficiency, whereas inference efficiency matters more for real-time perceptive control. R3 also asks for latency and onboard resource measurements against the main baselines.
\end{reviewcomment}
\Response The 56\% figure refers to \emph{peak GPU memory during training} relative to the Gallant voxel baseline. With the same GPU, parallel environment count, rollout length, PPO epochs, and batch settings, peak memory is 36,054\,MiB for Gallant and 15,870\,MiB for ADAPT: $(36{,}054-15{,}870)/36{,}054=55.98\%$. The respective iteration times are 34.6\,s and 11.3\,s, so ADAPT takes 32.7\% of Gallant's time under this setup.

The 98.6\% figure compares \emph{single-frame external perception inputs before encoding}. Gallant's $32\times32\times40$ voxel grid has 40,960 values; ADAPT's $21\times17$ horizontal elevation and $13\times17$ vertical distance maps have 578 values in total. Thus $(40{,}960-578)/40{,}960=98.59\%$.

For deployment efficiency, We added experiment for deploy lantency and computational resource usage. Table~IV(b) compares ADAPT with the main voxel baseline, Gallant, on the same local computer (Intel i7-12700H CPU and NVIDIA RTX 3060 Laptop GPU). Mean map-construction latency decreases from 64.06 to 41.64\,ms, policy GPU inference latency from 1.91 to 1.40\,ms, and perception-to-command-publication latency from 79.45 to 55.94\,ms (29.6\% lower). Sampled peak GPU memory decreases from 5,711 to 2,373\,MiB (58.4\% lower); mean whole-host CPU utilization decreases from 38.19\% to 17.76\%, and RAM usage from 10.66 to 8.04\,GiB.
\Changes Sec.~I restates the two efficiency quantities without attributing them to radius selection. Table~I in Sec.~II-A defines the pre-encoder sensory dimension, and Sec.~IV-B-3 derives the comparison from those dimensions and the training measurements in Table~IV. Table~IV(b) and Sec.~IV-C-3 report the runtime comparison, measurement boundaries, hardware, and trial setup on the local computer; these are not Jetson Orin NX measurements.
\Location{Abstract and Sec.~I, the paragraph beginning \textit{Empirical evaluation}; Sec.~II-A, Table~I caption; Sec.~IV-B-3, the two paragraphs immediately before Table~IV; Table~IV(a)--(b); and Sec.~IV-C-3 (Runtime Efficiency).}



\subsection*{AE Comment 2: Voxel baselines and adaptive reward}
\begin{reviewcomment}
Voxel-based representations are not inherently inferior and can have different computational trade-offs. Analyze whether the voxel-based baselines' performance gap arises from optimization difficulty, network capacity, training budget, representation redundancy, or implementation factors. Explain the role of the adaptive reward in learning the radius and clarify whether it encodes a hand-designed heuristic.
\end{reviewcomment}
\Response For the voxel baseline used in this study, insufficient look-ahead at high speeds leaves too little time to adjust motion smoothly and causes poorer traversal performance. In our high-speed tests, the robot decelerated abruptly after obstacles entered the sensing range and sometimes subsequently lost balance. ADAPT allows the policy to adjust the sensing range with its motion state, providing additional look-ahead for timely motion adjustment.

The adaptive reward contains radius smoothness and regularization terms. With current radius $r_t$, previous radius $r_{t-1}$, and initial radius $r_{\mathrm{init}}$, their contributions are $R_{\mathrm{smooth}}(t)=-\lambda_s|r_t-r_{t-1}|$ and $R_{\mathrm{reg}}(t)=-\lambda_r(r_t-r_{\mathrm{init}})^2$, where $\lambda_s=\lambda_r=0.1$ during training. Both terms are removed in the reward ablation, while the radius remains in the observation and action spaces. The terms discourage abrupt radius changes and drift from the initial radius; neither prescribes a target radius based on speed or obstacle distance. Our interpretation is that, without these terms, unstable radius choices can make future observations less consistent, especially at higher speeds. The ablation removes both terms together, so it does not isolate either term's causal contribution.
\Location{Sec.~III-B-3, Active Perception item, and Sec.~IV-B-4, the reward-ablation definition and high-speed result discussion. The voxel-baseline look-ahead explanation is provided in this response letter only; no additional manuscript change is claimed for that explanation.}

\subsection*{Reviewer 3, Comment 5: Deployment latency and onboard resources}
\begin{reviewcomment}
Table IV reports training efficiency, but inference efficiency is arguably more important for perceptive locomotion because training is performed offline, whereas perception and control must operate under strict real-time constraints during deployment. The authors are encouraged to report inference-time benchmarks, including map-construction latency, policy inference latency, total perception-to-control latency, and onboard computational resource usage. A comparison of these quantities across ADAPT and the main baselines would better support the paper's efficiency claims.
\end{reviewcomment}
\Response Thank you for this suggestion. We have extended Table~IV with runtime measurements for ADAPT and the Gallant voxel baseline and added the measurement setup and analysis in Sec.~IV-C-3. Both methods were deployed and benchmarked on the same local computer with an Intel i7-12700H CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 3060 Laptop GPU with 6 GiB VRAM.

Each method was tested five times on each of three terrain categories: flat ground, a single obstacle, and a complex environment, for 15 trials per method. Latency entries are equal-weight averages of per-trial means and P95s. Map latency spans the input callback to the first observed map-ready event; perception-to-publish latency spans that callback to the first command-publication call using the update. Policy GPU inference latency is measured with current-stream CUDA events. For ADAPT, each map-latency and perception-to-publish entry reports the larger statistic of its two map streams. CPU utilization and RAM usage are whole-host averages, and peak VRAM is the maximum sampled usage.

Relative to Gallant, ADAPT reduces mean map latency from 64.06 to 41.64 ms and mean perception-to-publish latency from 79.45 to 55.94 ms, a 29.6\% reduction for the latter. Mean policy GPU inference latency decreases from 1.91 to 1.40 ms, and sampled peak VRAM decreases from 5,711 to 2,373 MiB (58.4\% lower). Mean CPU utilization decreases from 38.19\% to 17.76\%, and RAM usage from 10.66 to 8.04 GiB.
\Changes Table~IV(b) and Sec.~IV-C-3 report the local-computer runtime comparison, measurement boundaries, hardware, and trial setup.

\subsection*{Reviewer 8, Comment 5: Adaptive reward}
\begin{reviewcomment}
The ablation shows that without the adaptive reward the policy fails to learn the optimal radius as speed increases. Do you have an explanation on why that happens? Which part of the adaptive reward is responsible for retrieving the optimal policy? Is the optimal reward basically enforcing a hand-designed heuristic? Understanding this could help better define the scope of the contribution.
\end{reviewcomment}
\Response The training reward for adaptive perception contains a smoothness term and a radius regularizer. With current radius $r_t$, previous radius $r_{t-1}$, and initial radius $r_{\mathrm{init}}$, their contributions to the total reward can be written as
\[
R_{\mathrm{smooth}}(t)=-\lambda_s|r_t-r_{t-1}|,\qquad
R_{\mathrm{reg}}(t)=-\lambda_r(r_t-r_{\mathrm{init}})^2,
\]
where $\lambda_s=\lambda_r=0.1$ during training. Thus, the combined radius-related contribution to the total reward is $-0.1|r_t-r_{t-1}|-0.1(r_t-r_{\mathrm{init}})^2$. Both contributions are removed in the reward ablation, while the radius remains in the observation and action spaces and all other reward terms are retained. The terms penalize abrupt radius changes and drift from the initial radius. Neither sets a target radius from speed or obstacle distance, and training uses no target-radius tracking reward. The regularizer encodes a preference for the initial radius, while allowing task rewards to favor a different radius when beneficial; it does not prescribe a speed-to-radius rule.

Our interpretation is that radius actions affect locomotion indirectly through future perception, which may be harder to learn than direct motor actions. Without regularization, early radius oscillation or drift can make observations less consistent; higher speeds may increase sensitivity to unsuitable or unstable sensing ranges. This is an interpretation of the ablation, not a separate causal measurement of either reward term.
\Changes Sec.~III-B-3 (Reward Functions), the Active Perception item, describes the smoothness and initial-radius deviation penalties. The exact formulas and the clarification that no speed- or obstacle-dependent target radius is prescribed are provided in this response. Sec.~IV-B-4 (Ablation Study on Adaptive Mechanism) states that the reward ablation removes both terms and interprets the high-speed result. The exact formulas and training weights are given directly above.
\Location{Sec.~III-B-3, Active Perception item; and Sec.~IV-B-4, the definition of w/o Adaptive Reward and the paragraph interpreting its high-speed performance. The detailed formulas and weights are supplied directly in this response.}
